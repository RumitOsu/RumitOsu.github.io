01Business review · E-commerce

More Sellers, Same Sales

Olist is a Brazilian company that lets small merchants sell on the country’s large shopping sites, and it once published the records of about 100,000 of its orders. I used them to write the review I would want to hand to a leadership team, which says what happened to sales, why it happened, and what to do before the next Black Friday.

The page opens with an executive summary that takes two minutes to read, and the evidence for each point follows it. The main finding is that the company kept adding sellers while its sales stood still, because the sellers it already had were each selling less. It closes with four recommendations, and each one comes with a first step, what it is worth and how sure I am.

02Experiments · Media

Pick the Winner

For two years the website Upworthy chose its headlines by trying them out on live visitors, and it later released the records of 32,487 of those tests. I used them to ask how often the winner of a test was really the best headline, and whether a person, a model or an AI could have picked it without running the test.

The page opens with a game in which you try to pick real winners yourself. After that it measures how much of a test result is luck, and it ends on the part that surprised me, which is that the tests were wrong about the best headline a third of the time and were still well worth running.

03Data story · Mongolia

Anatomy of a Dzud

A dzud is a Mongolian winter harsh enough to kill livestock by the million. I wanted to know why some winters take a quarter of the country’s animals while most take almost none, and how early anyone could tell which kind of winter was coming.

Thirty years of records gave a simple answer. A dry summer, long snow cover and a cold winter are each close to harmless alone, and they are dangerous together. The story walks you through it with a map you can explore, and most of the warning is already there by New Year, months before the animals die.

04Applied machine learning · Healthcare

Who Gets the Call?

One hundred patients leave the hospital today, and about ten of them will be back within a month. The nurse who follows up has time for twenty calls. I built a tool that picks those twenty, gives a reason for every name on the list, and turns a discharge note into a short guide for the call.

The page lets you try the nurse’s job yourself before it shows you what the model would have done. It also shows the part I found most interesting, which is that an experienced nurse’s rule of thumb gets almost as far as the model does.

I start from the decision.

In the readmission project the real question was never whether a given patient would come back. It was which twenty calls a nurse should make this morning. Asking it that way changed what I measured and what I built.

b

I check whether something simpler would do.

Before I trust a model, I compare it with the simplest rule a sensible person would use. In the dzud project three weather conditions forecast as well as a model with 46 inputs, so the three conditions are what I published.

c

I say what the work can’t do.

My readmission model is about level with an experienced nurse’s rule of thumb at choosing the list, and the page says so. I would rather show people where the limit is than have them find it later.
