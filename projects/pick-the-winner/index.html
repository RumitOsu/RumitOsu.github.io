Pick the Winner

For two years the website Upworthy settled every argument about a headline in the same way. It wrote several, showed each of them to a few thousand visitors, and kept whichever one drew the most clicks. It ran 32,487 of these tests and later gave the records to researchers.

I went through those records with two questions. How often was the winning headline really the best one? And could a person, a model or an AI have picked it without spending any visitors on a test? Before I tell you what I found, I’d like you to try the job yourself.

    Every pair in the game is a real test from the archive. I should admit that these are the easy ones: a pair only made it into the game if the test left almost no doubt about which headline was better. Most of the archive is not like that, and that is what the rest of this page is about.

    1 · The evidence behind a winner

    What one test looked like

    Here is a real test from December 2014. Every visitor saw the same story with the same picture, and only the headline changed from one visitor to the next.

    Upworthy would have gone with the first headline, and it looks like an easy call. But notice how few clicks sit behind each of those rates. Across the whole archive, the typical headline was shown to 3,117 people and clicked by 42 of them.

    Counts that small move around by luck. A headline that deserves 42 clicks will quite often collect 35 from one batch of visitors and 50 from the next, without anything about the headline having changed. So when one headline finishes a few clicks ahead of another, part of that lead belongs to the headline and part of it belongs to the particular visitors who happened to see it.

    You can watch this happen. Below are two made-up headlines whose true click rates I have fixed in advance, so that for once we know which one is better. Each small square is one complete test with fresh visitors, and it turns red when the worse headline comes out ahead.

    The starting position is a typical Upworthy test: about 3,100 visitors per headline, a click rate a little above 1%, and a true gap between the two headlines of 20%, which is an ordinary gap in this archive. Try a smaller gap, or ten times the visitors.

    Two things show up at the typical setting. The worse headline wins a noticeable share of the tests. And in the tests the better headline does win, its lead looks larger than it really is, because the tests where luck ran against it are exactly the ones it lost. Statisticians call this second effect the winner’s curse. Both problems fade when a test has more visitors, and both get worse when the two headlines are closer together.

    2 · Measuring the luck

    Two checks on the tests themselves

    The squares above come from arithmetic, and Upworthy’s visitors were real people. Before going further I wanted to measure how much luck there was in the real tests, in ways that don’t depend on any model of mine. The archive allows two.

    The same thing, tested against itself

    In 370 tests, the very same package was entered twice, with the same headline, the same picture and the same text. Any gap between two identical copies is luck by definition. The standard check that analysts use is built so that it calls about 5% of such pairs “significantly different” by accident. In the archive it called 7.1% of the 1,080 identical pairs different.

    So the real tests were a little noisier than the textbook assumes, by about 10%. One possible reason is that the same person could be counted more than once. It is a small correction, and I have applied it to every margin of error on this page. I mention it mainly because it is the kind of thing that is easy to skip, and skipping it would have made every result below look more certain than it is.

    The rematch

    The second check is the one I find most convincing. Upworthy sometimes tested a story more than once, and 2,269 pairs of headlines ended up facing each other in two separate tests. If winning a test means what it appears to mean, the headline that won the first meeting should usually win the second one too.

    How often the first winner won the rematch

    2,269 pairs that met twice

    “Clear” means the first test passed the standard significance check; “not clear” means it did not. The winner is simply the headline with the higher click rate. A coin would agree with the first test half the time.

    The first winner held up 64% of the time. When the first result had been clear, it held up 88% of the time, which is reassuring. But most first results were not clear, and for those the winner repeated only 60% of the time, which is not far from a coin.

    The winner’s lead shrank too. Where the first test had been clear, the winner appeared to be ahead by 101% on average. In the rematch the same headline was ahead by 57%.

    3 · The main question

    How often was the winner really the best?

    Rematches only cover pairs of headlines, and a typical test had four or five. To answer the question for whole tests I needed something the archive can never contain, which is the truth about each headline. So I built a copy of Upworthy on my computer in which the truth is known.

    The copy replays each of the 12,359 tests in which only the headline varied. Every replayed test has the same number of headlines as the real one, the same number of visitors and the same general click rate. The true differences between headlines are drawn to be as large as the archive says they are once the luck has been taken out. Then the visitors are sent through again, 300 times per test, and each time I can see whether the headline that came out on top was truly the best.

    A copy like this is only worth something if it behaves like the original, so I compared the two on three things the real archive can show.

    I did not expect these three numbers to sit together. One winner in three was not the best headline, and the scoreboard overstated the typical winner’s advantage by more than half. And yet the tests collected 82% of the gain that perfect knowledge would have delivered.

    The reason is that these tests rarely went badly wrong. When the best headline lost, it nearly always lost to one that was almost as good. Only 13% of winners were truly worse than the average headline in their own test. Being wrong about which headline is best turns out to be cheap, as long as you are roughly right about which ones are good.

    The same copy can replay the tests with less or more traffic than Upworthy really used, which answers a practical question: was all that traffic needed?

Upworthy was already on the flat part of the curve, and it could have run twice as many tests on the same audience at very little cost to each one.

    4 · Skipping the test

    Could anyone have called it without the test?

    A test costs visitors, and some of them are shown the weaker headlines. If the winner could be predicted from the words alone, the test would not be needed. So I held a contest. Each contestant is given the headlines of a test, and nothing else, and has to pick one.

    The contest is set up the way a prediction would really be used, looking forward in time. The contestants learn from the 17,755 tests that Upworthy started before August 2014. They are then scored on 4,559 later tests, all of them about stories that first appeared in September 2014 or after, so nothing they are scored on could have been seen before.

    There are seven contestants. The first is a rule of thumb that anyone could apply. Three are models of growing complexity that learn from past results, and a fifth combines those three. The last two are a language model of the kind behind today’s AI assistants, once on its own and once after being shown some past results.

    How much better than an average headline was each contestant’s pick?

    Reading the words gets a contestant further than I expected. The combined model had never seen any of these stories, and the headline it picked was clicked 17% more often than the average headline in its test. That is 56% of what perfect knowledge would deliver, and it costs no visitors at all. A real test has to run on roughly a quarter of Upworthy’s traffic before it does as well.

    The test still wins, at +23%. It also has an advantage that the chart cannot show. The models learned what Upworthy’s readers liked in 2013 and the first half of 2014, and they are only as good as that taste is stable. A test asks today’s readers.

    The language model is the surprise in the other direction. Given no examples, its picks did 4.3% better than average, which puts it level with the checklist model and a long way behind the others. Showing it twelve past tests together with how they came out made no real difference (3.5%). Its taste also had little in common with the combined model’s. The two chose the same headline in 23% of tests, and two contestants picking at random would agree in 21%.

    I would not read that as “language models are bad at headlines”. This is a small model that runs on a laptop, and a larger one would probably do better. What the result does show is where the skill comes from. The plain models above it are far simpler, but they learned from 17,755 of Upworthy’s own tests what this particular audience clicked on. The language model knows what headlines in general look like, and that turned out to be worth much less than knowing the readers.

    One caution for anyone who repeats this. My first version of the contest looked better than the one above: the combined model called 84% of the clear pairs, against 80% here. The archive comes divided at random into three files, and I had trained on two of them and scored on the third. But Upworthy usually tested a story several times within a day or two, often with lightly edited headlines, so retests of the same story ended up on both sides and the model was partly being asked to remember. Dividing the tests by time closes that door, and it is also how a model like this would really be used.

    The strongest 18 in each direction, after leaving out names and words tied to a single news story.

    The left side is the style Upworthy became known for. These headlines point at something without saying what it is (this, here’s, what). They promise a moment (then, asked, caught, response), and they tell you how little of your time it will take (seconds, minutes). They speak of a person as her or him and leave the name out.

    The right side interests me more. It is full of what the stories were actually about: rights, equality, community, the economy, the future. Headlines that named the cause tended to lose to headlines that kept it back. Question marks, colons and exclamation marks counted against a headline as well.

    None of this is advice. It is a record of what one audience clicked on over two particular years, and in August 2014 Facebook announced that it would start showing fewer headlines that withhold what a story is about. A model trained on these words would have gone on recommending them.

    6 · So what

    What I would tell the editor

    The point of all this is a decision that someone has to make every day, so here is what I would say to the person making it.

    1. Keep running the tests.

      Nothing else in the contest matched them. A test delivers a headline about 23% better than average, and the best model I could build delivers about 17%. The tests’ real weakness, which is naming the wrong winner one time in three, costs very little, because the wrong winner is nearly always a good headline too.

    2. Don’t repeat the winner’s lead.

      A winner that appears to be 37% ahead of the average headline is really about 23% ahead. If the number on the scoreboard goes into a report or a forecast, the person reading it should be told that about 39% of it is luck.

    3. Spend less traffic on each test, and run more tests.

      Half the traffic still collects 73% of the possible gain, against 82% at full traffic. The visitors saved would do more good in a second test on another story than they do in sharpening the first.

    4. Let a model decide what gets tested.

      The model is not good enough to replace the test, but it is good enough to prepare it. The headline the combined model liked least was clicked 16% less often than the average headline in its test, and it turned out to be the best one only 11% of the time. Dropping it before the test starts would spare visitors from the weakest option and leave more of them for the headlines that have a chance.

    7 · Limits

    What this can’t tell you

    A click is not the same as a good headline. The tests counted who clicked. They did not count who read to the end, who shared the story, or who felt misled once they arrived. A headline can win the click and lose the reader, and nothing here can see that.

    This is one website at one moment. Upworthy’s visitors in 2013 to 2015 came mostly from Facebook, and the style that worked on them wore out quickly. The words in section 5 are a record of that audience and should not be read as advice for today. What should carry over is the arithmetic of small counts, which is the same for any test that ends with a few dozen clicks, sign-ups or purchases on each side.

    The copy of Upworthy is a model. It matches the real archive on everything I could check, including rematches it had never seen, but the 67% and the 82% come from the copy. They are estimates, and they rest on my description of how large the true differences between headlines are.

    I only replayed tests where the headline alone varied. That is 12,359 of the 32,487 tests. The rest also changed the picture or the text shown with the story, which makes it impossible to say what a win was a win for.

    The contestants could be stronger. The language model is a small one that runs on a laptop, and I did not train it on this data. A larger model, or one trained on these tests, would probably do better than either version here.

    8 · Method

    How this was made

    Data
    The Upworthy Research Archive, released by J. Nathan Matias, Kevin Munger, Marianne Aubin Le Quere and Charles Ebersole (Scientific Data, 2021) under a CC BY 4.0 licence. It holds 150,817 packages from 32,487 tests run between January 2013 and April 2015. The archive comes in three files: one for exploring, one for confirming, and one held back. I used them in that order for the noise measurements, and divided the tests by time for the contest, for the reason given in section 4.
    Measuring the luck
    The noise comes from 1,080 identical pairs. Its spread is measured from the middle half of their results, so that a few broken tests cannot move it. The size of true headline effects is fitted on the first two files as a mixture of bell curves of different widths, and checked against the third file and against 2,269 rematches that played no part in the fitting.
    The copy
    12,359 headline-only tests, each replayed 300 times at nine levels of traffic, in Python with NumPy.
    The contest
    Models are logistic regressions on the difference between two headlines, built with scikit-learn. The meaning model uses sentence embeddings from all-mpnet-base-v2. The language model is Qwen3 with 8 billion parameters, run locally through Ollama, with the headlines shuffled for every test because it turned out to favour certain positions in a list. It was scored on a random 1,500 of the scored tests. Tests are grouped into stories by shared headlines, pictures and opening lines, and no story appears on both sides of the contest. As a further check I removed every scored test that had a headline closely resembling one from the learning period, and the results did not change.
    Tools
    Python, pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, sentence-transformers and Ollama for the analysis. The page is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no libraries, and the simulator in section 1 runs in your browser.
